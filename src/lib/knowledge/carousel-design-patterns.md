# CAROUSEL DESIGN PATTERNS (from real high-performing LinkedIn carousels)

Catalogued from published carousels by Supergrow, Sprout Social, Career Sherpa,
DocHipo, SocialBee, Hootsuite, LinkedIn's own page, Social Media Examiner and
others. These are the layouts that actually recur — not theory.

Each entry says what the slide DOES, what copy shape it needs, and which
`slideTemplate` produces it. Choose the pattern that fits the idea; do not default
to the same one every slide. A deck that alternates layouts reads as designed; a
deck of eight identical card grids reads as a text dump.

---

## 1. STATEMENT COVER → `title`

A single bold claim filling most of the slide, on a deep colour panel, with a
short subtitle underneath. Seen on "WE ARE HIRING", "30 POST IDEAS & EXAMPLES",
"WHAT TO POST ON LINKEDIN".

- Title: 3–6 words. Big enough to read as a thumbnail in the feed.
- Subtitle: one line of context, under 90 characters.
- This slide sells the swipe. If it does not create curiosity, nothing after it
  gets read.

## 2. TILE GRID → `iconGrid`

6–9 small labelled tiles in a grid, each one item, each with an icon. Seen on
"24 Ideas for What To Share On LinkedIn" and "Growth Mindset — 15 Ways".

- Each tile: **2–4 words only.** No sentences. `Ask A Question`, `Industry News`,
  `Case Study`.
- Give 6 or 9 items — a full grid. Five looks broken.
- Best for: menus of options, idea lists, category overviews.

## 3. NUMBERED LIST → `numbered`

Numerals in filled colour circles down the left, each with a bold label and one
supporting line. Seen on "10 LinkedIn Post Ideas for Job Seekers".

- 4–5 items. Write each as `Label — one supporting sentence.`
- Best for: steps in order, ranked lists, frameworks.

## 4. BEFORE / AFTER → `twoColumn`

Two panels side by side. The left is the losing approach in grey with ✗ marks;
the right is the winning one in brand colour with ✓ marks.

- 3–5 bullets per side, each under 60 characters.
- Both columns need the SAME number of bullets or the slide looks lopsided.
- Best for: old way vs new way, myth vs reality, wrong vs right.

## 5. BIG STATISTIC → `bigStat`

One number carrying the whole slide, inside a ring, with a short caps label and a
line of context. Seen on "54% of Gen Zs and 48% of millennials…".

- The stat must be REAL and from the creator's own evidence or a cited source.
- Label: under 30 characters, uppercase.
- Best for: the one number that proves the argument.

## 6. PROCESS STEPS → `timeline`

Stages connected down a rail, each a numbered node. Seen on "Choose a business
idea → Build your product → Test and review" and the circular content-marketing
diagram.

- 4–5 stages. Each `Stage name — what happens.`
- Best for: sequences over time, pipelines, week-by-week plans.

## 7. PULL QUOTE → `quote`

One quotable line, oversized, on a deep colour panel with a large quotation mark.
Seen on the UK Business Advisors and Miro testimonial slides.

- Under 120 characters. It has to be the single best sentence in the deck.
- Use once per deck, in the back half, as a change of pace.

## 8. CHECKLIST → `worksheet`

Alternating tinted rows, each with a check icon. Seen on the "TL;DR checklist"
pattern and self-audit carousels.

- 3–5 items, each a complete prompt or question the reader can act on.
- Best for: takeaways, self-assessment, "do these five things".

## 9. SCRIPT / TEMPLATE CARD → `templateCard`

A bordered card containing copy the reader can steal verbatim — an outreach
message, a resignation line, an email opener.

- Use `**bold**` for the parts they fill in or the key line.
- Best for: the "save this post" slide. Highest save rate of any layout.

## 10. SECTION BREAK → `divider`

Two or three words on a full colour panel. No body.

- Use between major sections of a long deck, never twice in a row.

## WHAT THE 2026 BENCHMARKS SAY

Measured data, not opinion. Sources listed at the end of this file.

- **Carousels beat text posts roughly 3.7x on engagement** and multi-slide posts
  pull ~3.4x the reach of single-image posts. Document posts are the highest-
  performing organic format on the platform. Median B2B engagement rate sits
  around 5.7%.
- **8–10 slides is the target.** Consensus range is 6–12; past 12 there is
  meaningful drop-off, and past 15 completion falls by about 40%.
- **The cover carries ~80% of the outcome.** If slide 1 does not stop the scroll,
  nothing after it is read.
- **20–30% of readers leave between slide 1 and slide 2** — the single biggest
  leak in any carousel. It climbs toward 50% by mid-deck when the content stalls.
- **Type minimums on a 1080px canvas: 48px headings, 24px body.** The rule that
  follows from this is "less text, bigger font, more slides". Cramming paragraphs
  onto a slide destroys mobile readability and completion rate.

### The two rules that follow from that data

**1. The cover states the PAYOFF, not the subject.**
Weak covers name the topic. Strong covers name what the reader gets.

- Weak: `Edge Deployment and Quantization`
- Strong: `Your Edge Model Does Not Need INT8`

**2. Slide 2 must immediately pay off the cover's promise.**
Do not use slide 2 for context, background or a table of contents. Deliver the
proof — the number, the contrast, the answer. If the payoff waits until slide 5,
most of the audience has already gone. This is why the rhythm below puts a
`bigStat`, `twoColumn` or `quote` in position two.

## THE PILLAR DECK — a second shape worth knowing

Alongside the alternating rhythm below, there is a second structure that recurs in
high-performing decks: **N pillars, M points each.** Will McTighe's "10 Rules to
10X Your Output" runs three pillars — FOCUS, DECISIONS, REPETITION — with three
concrete tactics under each.

Why it works: the reader can hold three things in their head. Ten unstructured
rules is a list to skim; three named pillars is a model to remember and repeat.

The shape:

1. `title` — the numbered promise
2. `quote` or `bigStat` — the belief being refuted
3. `divider` — PILLAR ONE
4. `numbered` — its 3 tactics
5. `divider` — PILLAR TWO
6. `numbered` — its 3 tactics
7. `divider` — PILLAR THREE
8. `numbered` — its 3 tactics
9. `templateCard` or `quote` — the call to action

This is the one case where repeated templates are correct: the repetition IS the
structure, and the dividers between them carry the pillar names. Use three
pillars, occasionally four. Never five — the model stops being memorable.

### Cover variant: the numbered promise

`10 Rules to 10X Your Output` — a count plus a specific outcome. It still obeys
the payoff rule (the reader learns what they get, not what the topic is), and the
number sets an expectation of completeness that pulls people through the deck.

Only use a number you actually deliver. Promising ten and shipping six is the
fastest way to lose a reader in the back half.

### Open by refuting a belief the reader holds

McTighe's hook is not a claim, it is a correction: *"10x more output = 10x more
hours"* — stated as something he believed, then dismantled. Naming the reader's
current belief before replacing it is stronger than asserting your own, because it
tells them they are about to be wrong about something. That is a hard swipe to
resist.

## DECK RHYTHM — the part most decks get wrong

Alternate. The published decks above almost never repeat a layout back to back.

A strong 9-slide shape:

1. `title` — the payoff, not the topic
2. `bigStat`, `twoColumn` or `quote` — pay the promise off IMMEDIATELY
3. `cardGrid` or `iconGrid` — the overview
4. `twoColumn` — the contrast
5. `divider` — a beat
6. `numbered` or `timeline` — the method
7. `worksheet` — what they do next
8. `templateCard` — the thing they save
9. `quote` or `title` — the call to action

Rules:
- Never use the same template twice in a row.
- At most two colour-panel slides (`title`, `divider`, `quote`) in an 8-slide deck
  besides the cover — they are punctuation, not content.
- Every content slide carries ONE idea. If it needs two, split it.
- The last slide always asks for something specific: follow, comment a keyword,
  save, or connect.

## COPY LENGTH BUDGETS

| Slot | Budget |
|---|---|
| Cover title | 45 characters |
| Section break | 25 characters |
| Pull quote | 120 characters |
| Content slide heading | 55 characters |
| Card / numbered item | 110 characters |
| Tile in a tile grid | 24 characters |
| Section tag | 30 characters, uppercase |
| Stat figure | 8 characters |

Text over budget is shrunk to fit, which makes one slide look different from the
rest — and shrinking body copy below ~24px on a 1080px canvas breaks mobile
readability outright. Write to the budget instead. When a slide will not fit,
split it into two slides rather than shrinking the type: more slides is the
cheaper trade, right up to about 10.

# THE MINIMAL SWIPE DECK (default renderer)

Modelled on the format that consistently wins: Will McTighe's "10 Rules to 10X
Your Output" (12 pages), and the same shape used by Justin Welsh, Jasmin Alić and
Sahil Bloom. It is the house style for this product because it is the one that
survives being viewed at thumbnail size on a phone, and because it reads as a
person's deck rather than an agency's.

## The system

Warm or cool off-white ground. ONE accent colour. No icons, no illustration, no
charts. Hierarchy and spacing do all the work.

Every content slide is exactly four elements, in this order:

1. **Numbered chip** — the rule/step number in a rounded accent square. It tells
   the reader where they are without a progress bar.
2. **Title** — 2 lines max, bold, sentence case. The rule itself, not a topic
   label. "Cut 2 Tasks Before Adding 1", not "Task Management".
3. **Body card** — a soft tinted card holding 3–4 SHORT PARAGRAPHS. This is the
   part most decks get wrong.
4. **Takeaway card** — a second, warmer card holding ONE bolded sentence: the
   rule or "so what". This is what makes the deck skimmable.

Footer on every slide: a dashed swipe arrow bottom-left, the creator's name
bottom-right. Cover slide is the inverse — full-bleed dark ground, oversized
title, accent rule, "N SLIDES" pill, "SWIPE →".

## Body copy shape — the thing that separates good from generic

Write standalone paragraphs of 1–2 complete sentences, in the voice of someone
talking. NOT bullet fragments, NOT headings with colons.

The recurring rhythm across winning decks is a four-beat confession:

1. **What I used to do** — "I used to treat breaks like a reward for finishing
   the work."
2. **Why it broke** — "But when the work never really ends, that usually means
   no break at all."
3. **What I do now** — "Now I step away every couple of hours. 10 minutes to walk
   or grab food. Nothing with a screen."
4. **The result** — "I come back with more focus and usually finish the next task
   faster."

Then the takeaway lands the rule: "Working longer is not the same as getting more
done. Step away before your focus disappears."

The confession structure is why these decks feel earned rather than lectured. A
slide that opens on advice ("Take regular breaks to stay productive") is the
failure mode.

## Takeaway rules

- One sentence, max ~110 characters. It renders in its own card.
- Never restate the title. The title names the rule; the takeaway explains why
  the rule is true.
- Written as a principle, not a summary: "Most to-do lists fail from addition.
  Make every new task earn its place."

## Infographic slides

The one place this style adds a visual is the `bigStat` slide: a single figure at
huge scale in the accent colour, a small uppercase label under it, takeaway
below. Use it once or twice per deck, on the number that carries the argument.
`twoColumn` is the other structural slide — before/after, busy/productive,
what-most-do/what-works. Both are still type-only.

## Why this fits a job-search audience

Hiring managers scan. A deck where each slide states a rule, proves it with a
short personal story, and closes with a one-line principle can be read in full at
speed, or skimmed by title + takeaway alone and still deliver. Decorative
carousels read as marketing; this reads as someone who knows their work.
