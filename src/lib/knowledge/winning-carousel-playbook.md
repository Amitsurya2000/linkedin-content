# THE WINNING CAROUSEL PLAYBOOK

What separates a carousel that gets saved from one that gets scrolled past.
Compiled from 2026 benchmark data (Socialinsider, Morphica, Omnicreator) and
hook libraries built on creator performance data.

Read this as the standard the deck is judged against, not as suggestions.

---

## 1. THE NUMBERS THAT DEFINE "WINNING"

| Metric | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Save rate | **8%+ is viral.** 4–6% is high-performing. |
| Engagement rate | Carousels 24.42% vs text posts 6.67% — roughly 4× |
| Saves | 1.8× a single-image post |
| Slide count | **6–10.** Enough value to earn a save, short enough to finish. |

**Saves are the metric that compounds.** The 2026 feed weights saved content
heavily, and a save keeps paying out for weeks. Everything below optimises for
saves, not likes.

**92% of feed swipes are decided before the reader gets past the first line.**
That is the entire game. A deck with a weak slide 1 does not get a second
chance from good slides 2–10.

---

## 2. THE HOOK IS 92% OF THE OUTCOME

A specific-outcome hook outperforms a topic hook by **3–7× on saves**.

| Weak (topic) | Strong (outcome) |
|---|---|
| "Cold email tips" | "The cold email that booked 41 demos in 30 days" |
| "Edge deployment and quantization" | "Your edge model does not need INT8" |
| "Month-end close best practices" | "Our close took 5 days. Now it takes 36 hours." |

The rule: **name the payoff, and put the number in it.** If the hook could sit on
anyone's post, it is a topic, not a hook.

### The 10 hook categories

Pick the one that fits the material. Do not blend two.

1. **Curiosity gap** — opens a loop only the deck can close.
   *"What I learned after [specific outcome] that nobody talks about."*
2. **Contrarian** — attacks a belief the audience holds.
   *"Everyone says [popular belief]. They're wrong."*
3. **Listicle** — a promised count, delivered exactly.
   *"[N] [topic] habits that changed my [outcome]."*
4. **Data** — a statistic leads.
   *"[X]% of [audience] [behaviour]. Most don't realise it."*
5. **Story** — a personal arc with a low point and a turn.
   *"[Duration] ago I [low point]. Today [outcome]. Here's the playbook."*
6. **Question** — names a frustration as a question.
   *"Why does your [effort] feel like [pain] but produce [low outcome]?"*
7. **Problem-agitation** — names the pain and raises the stakes.
   *"If your [topic] still [pain point], stop reading and fix this first."*
8. **Promise** — a transformation guarantee.
   *"After this carousel you'll never [bad habit] again."*
9. **Negative** — a warning, a "stop doing this".
   *"Stop [popular practice]. It's costing you [outcome]."*
10. **Authority** — credibility first, then the goods.
    *"I've [outcome] for [N] [audience]. Here's the playbook I never share."*

## 3. NAME YOUR FRAMEWORK

Named frameworks drive saves harder than anything else, because a name makes the
idea **reusable** — the reader saves it to apply later, and can repeat it to a
colleague without re-reading.

- "The 3-C Framework for Positioning"
- "The RICE Method for Content Prioritisation"
- "The 36-Hour Close"

If a deck teaches a repeatable method, **give it a name and use that name on the
cover**. An unnamed method is advice; a named one is a tool.

---

## 4. PROBLEM → SOLUTION → RESULT

The structural spine underneath most high-performing decks:

1. **Problem** — the specific, recognisable pain. Named in the reader's words.
2. **Agitation** — what it costs. Hours, money, missed outcomes. One slide.
3. **Solution** — the mechanism. Named, concrete, repeatable.
4. **Result** — the number it produced. Real, never invented.

A deck that skips agitation reads as a lecture. One that skips the result reads
as theory.

---

## 5. THE SCORECARD

Every deck is graded on these seven before it ships. A deck failing any of the
first three is not shippable regardless of how the rest score.

| # | Test | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Outcome hook** | Slide 1 names a topic, not a payoff. No number, no tension. |
| 2 | **Slide 2 delivers** | Slide 2 is background, a contents list, or an introduction. |
| 3 | **Real specifics** | No named tool, figure, failure or trade-off anywhere. Generic advice. |
| 4 | **Save-worthy** | Nothing here is worth returning to. No framework, checklist or template. |
| 5 | **One idea per slide** | A slide needs two sentences of setup before its point. |
| 6 | **Takeaway lands** | The takeaway restates the title instead of explaining why it's true. |
| 7 | **CTA earns it** | Closing slide says "thanks for reading" rather than making one specific ask. |

---

## 6. THE FAILURE MODES

The four ways a deck reliably loses, in order of how often they happen:

1. **Topic-as-hook.** Slide 1 announces the subject. 92% of the audience is gone.
2. **Advice voice.** "Take regular breaks to stay productive." Anyone could write
   it, so nobody saves it. The fix is the confession: what you used to do, why it
   broke, what you do now, what it produced.
3. **Padding to hit a count.** A promised 10 with 6 real points and 4 restatements.
   Better to promise 6.
4. **Invented numbers.** The one unforgivable failure — a fabricated figure is a
   liability the creator has to defend in the comments and cannot.
